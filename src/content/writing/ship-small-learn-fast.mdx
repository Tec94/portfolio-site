A hackathon makes you ship small whether you want to or not. What it doesn't do is tell you which small thing to build, and I've gotten that wrong more than once.

## Pick one question first

The way I think about it now, your first release should answer one question you can't answer just by talking about it. Everything else can wait.

## The boring parts are part of the slice

It's really tempting to skip sign-in, saving, loading states, and error handling, because they're not the fun part and they don't show up in the demo. But if you leave them out, you're testing a demo, and people give a demo way more slack than they'd give a tool they actually depend on.

## Write down what people did

Before planning the next version, write down what people actually did with this one. Your backlog is mostly guesses you made before anyone touched the thing. What people did shows you which of those guesses were right.

## Where this stops working

This is the part I didn't get at first. Shipping small works great when you're building something people can't do at all right now. It kind of falls apart when there are already good tools for it.

Linear wrote about this in [Rethinking the startup MVP](https://linear.app/now/rethinking-the-startup-mvp-building-a-competitive-product). If someone can compare your thin first version to a mature product, they'll just pick the mature product, and your test doesn't tell you anything.

So in a crowded space, I think your first slice has to beat the existing tool at one specific thing your users care about. It's still one slice. You just have to build that slice a lot better.
